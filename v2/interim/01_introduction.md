# 1 Introduction

Smart homes are increasingly run by software that the household controls itself. Home Assistant is a widely used open-source platform of this kind: it runs on a small computer inside the home and controls its lights, heating, cameras, alarms and door locks, and more than 669,000 installations report to its optional analytics (Home Assistant, n.d.-b).

Much of the platform's appeal comes from integrations, the plug-in modules that connect it to a particular device or online service. Beyond those shipped with the platform, thousands are written by the community and installed through a community store, and more than half of the reporting installations use it. Unlike apps on a phone, these integrations ask for no permissions. Each one runs inside the platform itself, with the same access as the platform, so an integration that only reports the weather could, if it were faulty or malicious, also unlock the front door. Most community integrations are small projects with few users, the least likely to be reviewed by anyone, and the platform leaves the risk of installing them to the owner (Home Assistant, 2021a), who has no way to see or limit what they do.

This project builds and evaluates a framework that confines each community integration to what its owner has approved, without changing the integration's code or the platform. Each integration runs in its own isolated environment, a sandbox, and every action it takes on the real home passes through a separate component that checks it against the approved permissions. The owner approves those permissions in the platform's own interface, and every action is recorded in a log that cannot be altered unnoticed.

The framework was evaluated on a Raspberry Pi 5, comparing real community integrations with and without confinement. Two published attack techniques by a hostile integration worked against an ordinary installation and found nothing under confinement, and the project's own attacks recovered no planted secret; all 75 integrations tested that loaded normally also loaded when confined, and the device ran at least 17 confined integrations at once, about four times the average number installed by those who use the community store.
