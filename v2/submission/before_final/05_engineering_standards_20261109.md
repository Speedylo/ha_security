# 5. Engineering Standards

A standard shapes a project only where the work follows its structure or can be checked against its requirements, so this section names only standards of that kind. For each, it states what the standard requires, how the project meets it and, where the project departs from it, why.

## 5.1 Risk management and threat modelling

The threat model follows ISO 31000 [20], which fixes the order of a risk-management process: establish the scope and context, identify risks, analyse and evaluate them, then treat, monitor and record them. The project keeps that order, and Section 8 shows its main consequence: because risks were assessed before any control was designed, the model produced a requirement, the bound on resources (S9), that no earlier requirement contained. ISO 31000 prescribes no technique for identifying risks, however, so STRIDE [21] fills that step, applied to each of the six trust boundaries in turn. The standard also supplies no impact scale, and the project departs from generic scales deliberately. Impact is derived from the value of the household's assets, because no general scale has a level for defeating a safety function in a dwelling. The model is drawn with OWASP Threat Dragon [27].

## 5.2 Least privilege

The standard closest to the platform's problem is ETSI EN 303 645 [28], the European baseline for consumer IoT security, whose scope names "connected home automation and alarm systems, especially their gateways and hubs". Its provision 5.6-7 states that "software should run with least necessary privileges", and NIST SP 800-53 states the same principle as control AC-6 for processes acting on behalf of users [29]. Home Assistant's integration model is the opposite of this provision, since every integration runs with all of the platform's privileges. The framework meets it in two ways. Each integration receives only what its manifest declares, and most of what it does not declare is absent from its view rather than refused on request. The framework also applies the principle to itself, because the mediator acts on the platform as a user without administrator rights. One departure is stated: the system-call filter denies 29 dangerous calls instead of allowing only the calls an integration needs, because the namespaces have already removed almost everything those calls could reach (Section 9.2).

## 5.3 Audit

Attribution depends on the record surviving, and audit standards say what a system may do when it cannot record. Common Criteria component FAU_STG.4 [30] requires a design to choose, for when its audit trail is full, between ignoring audited events, preventing them and overwriting the oldest records, and NIST SP 800-53 control AU-5 [29] requires an alert and leaves further action to the organisation, with shutdown on failure as an optional enhancement, AU-5(4). Neither standard prefers stopping, but both recognise it as legitimate, and the framework chooses it because losing records would break requirement S7. It applies that choice at two scales. A decision is written to disk before it is acted upon, and a failed write stops the mediator, so every channel closes and no unrecorded action can occur. A single sandbox that floods the mediator with refused requests is stopped instead, after 100 refusals within ten seconds, so that its neighbours keep running while every refusal is still recorded.

## 5.4 Network addresses

Two address standards define what the egress proxy refuses. RFC 1918 [31] reserves the private ranges that home routers assign to the devices in a house, and RFC 6598 [32] reserves 100.64.0.0/10 for carrier-grade NAT, a range that overlay networks such as Tailscale also use. The proxy refuses both, together with loopback and link-local addresses, unless the manifest separately grants the home network. A grant for a public host therefore cannot be turned into a route to the router or to the reference device. RFC 6598 was added after the cut-off, when the writing of Section 9 found that the first check accepted that range (finding F58).
