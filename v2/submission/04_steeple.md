# 4. STEEPLE Analysis

The problem of Section 2 lies in software, but its causes and consequences extend beyond it. This section examines the project's context under seven headings, separating in each what the evaluation measured from what is presented as argument.

## 4.1 Social

The asset at risk is a household rather than a computer. The platform's entities record occupancy and the hours a house stands empty, and its actions open locks and garage doors [2], so the harm of a hostile integration is surveillance of a household and physical access to it, neither of which can be reversed. The platform assigns this risk to whoever installs an integration [3], yet every occupant is exposed, including those who neither chose it nor could read its code. The project's social contribution is correspondingly narrow: authority that was invisible becomes declared, and is shown in plain language in the platform's interface before an integration starts. A declaration makes the risk knowable; it does not make an integration safe.

## 4.2 Technological

The platform's extension model sets the technological problem: every integration runs inside the platform's own process with its full authority. Browsers solved the same problem by requiring a permission-based extension model while their platforms were still new [14]. Home Assistant cannot, because its integrations already exist, so the question is whether confinement can be added afterwards. The project shows that it can, with isolation facilities already in the Linux kernel of a commodity device and no change to the code of the integrations or the platform. Such a retrofit depends on platform internals, however: the first real integration run confined could not start the platform's recommended HTTP client [22] until the framework was adapted. The compatibility results therefore hold for the release measured, 2026.8.3, and must be rechecked for each new one.

## 4.3 Economic

The economics of the ecosystem decide which solutions are possible. The median community integration has 20 installations, and 3,095 of 4,220 have fewer than 100 [1], so most are volunteer projects with no revenue to fund a rewrite. A control that asked their authors to change their code would not be adopted, which is why requirement C1 leaves it unmodified. The cost moves instead to the device and to the framework's maintainers. On the device it is small: the reference hardware holds 17 to 43 sandboxes, depending on how much of the platform each integration needs loaded beside it, against a mean of 4.02 integrations per installation [1]. What remains is labour, since each integration still needs a manifest, every evaluation manifest was written by hand, and the framework must follow each platform release. Adoption depends on that effort rather than on hardware.

## 4.4 Environmental

Home automation has an environmental dimension because a household's devices can outlive the services that support them. When a vendor withdraws its cloud service, a device that depends on it stops working, and a self-hosted platform can keep that device in use, often through a community integration; this benefit is argued here rather than measured. A security measure could undo it in two ways, by requiring additional hardware or by breaking the integrations that keep devices working. The framework avoids both. It runs on the same single-board computer as the platform, using isolation facilities the kernel already provides, and the heavier alternatives, gVisor and microVMs, were rejected partly because their memory cost per sandbox would not fit that device. Confinement also caused no import-level failure across 75 integrations. The framework therefore adds security without adding hardware or retiring the devices the platform controls.

## 4.5 Political

Responsibility for what a community integration does falls between the parties that might carry it. The platform disclaims it [9]. Regulation might be expected to fill that gap, but the Cyber Resilience Act [23] places its duties on commercial suppliers, and its recital 18 states that providing non-monetised open-source software is not a commercial activity. The authors, volunteers whose median integration has 20 installations [1], have neither the obligation nor the means. The risk therefore rests with the user, the one party with no means to act on it [3]. The project's contribution is to give the user that means without waiting for the question to be settled: a bound that is declared, enforced and recorded holds whoever is eventually made responsible. That allocation may yet change, since the platform's own hardware is designed by a company associated with the project [24], and a platform held responsible would need exactly such a bound.

## 4.6 Legal

Where the law imposes a duty, it presupposes evidence that a stock installation cannot produce. Since 11 September 2026, Article 14 of the Cyber Resilience Act has required a manufacturer to report an actively exploited vulnerability in its product within 24 hours of becoming aware of it [23]. Becoming aware requires knowing what was exploited, yet an integration's calls carry no user and pass no permission check [10], so extensions "are free to perform actions without being authenticated, reducing the ability to audit logs and locate malicious extensions" [25]. No household, author or manufacturer can show what an integration accessed. The framework's decision record (requirement S7) supplies that evidence: each request, granted or refused, is recorded with the integration and the clause that decided it, on disk before the action is taken.

## 4.7 Ethical

The central ethical question is who controls data about the people in a home. An integration that can read occupancy and the hours a house stands empty can send that record anywhere, and the occupants, guests and children it describes are observed without knowing it. Nor do they consent, since one person approves an integration for the whole household, and such approval is weak even on one's own behalf: of Android users shown install-time permissions, 17% paid attention to them and only 3% of those surveyed answered all three comprehension questions correctly [26]. The framework returns part of that control to the household. An integration reads only the entities it declared and connects only to the hosts it named, and each entity mirrored in and each connection opened is a recorded decision, so what was exposed, and to where, can be known. The control is partial, however: the mediator decides where data goes but never sees what is sent to a permitted host (T09, retained), and without a user study there is no evidence that the person approving a grant has understood it.
