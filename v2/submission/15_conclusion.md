# 15. Conclusion

Home Assistant loads community integrations into its own process with the platform's full authority, and the controls that exist act on the likelihood that hostile code arrives rather than on its impact. This project built the missing control, through which an integration's authority is declared, enforced while it runs and recorded, under three constraints that rule out the mechanisms of prior work: the integrations' code could not be changed, the platform could not be changed, and the cooperation of authors could not be assumed. Its central claim was that unmodified integrations can be confined at a cost worth paying, and the claim was divided into three assertions, each with a falsifier recorded before anything was measured.

None of the three falsifiers fired. Confinement is effective: the published technique of walking the garbage collector to another integration's credential succeeds unconfined and finds nothing confined, because the credential is in another process, and the 2021 file-theft pattern recovers both planted secrets unconfined and neither confined. Confinement is compatible: across 75 real community integrations on two corpora, it caused no failure to load that did not also occur without it, and thirteen integrations ran confined end to end through their own setup, each producing the value its own code implies. Confinement is affordable: the mediator runs about sixty times clear of realistic demand, and a Raspberry Pi 5 holds at least 17 sandboxes against a mean of 4.02 community integrations per installation. The fourth falsifier, which concerned the evaluation itself, did fire twice during development, and the verification campaign that followed found six statements in the report that were wrong or unsupported; all were corrected and all are reported.

The claim holds within bounds that the results do not remove. Every attack was written within the project, and the code review that found a route out of the sandbox missed by every test shows why an independent adversary is the first item of future work. The thirteen integrations executed end to end are a named set, limited by the account credentials and device addresses most integrations require. Every manifest in the evaluation was written by hand, so the integrations' code is unmodified but the cost of supplying manifests at scale is unmeasured. All results describe one platform release on one device, and four High residual risks remain, each stated: an over-broad declaration, abuse of declared authority, exfiltration to a permitted host, and a mediator whose credential still reaches every device.

What the framework changes is the nature of an integration's authority. Authority that was total and invisible becomes declared before an integration starts, bounded while it runs and recorded each time it is used, so a household can know what each integration may do and what it has done. A durable fix, however, belongs to the platform. Because a check inside the platform's process can be reached by the code it restricts [5], Home Assistant would have to place community integrations behind a process boundary itself, and to make its existing per-entity policy configurable so that the mediator's own credential could be narrowed. This project supplies the evidence such a change would need: what confinement costs, what a declaration must express and which threats it closes.

The method contributed as much as the mechanism. Every prediction was committed before the measurement that tested it, and 205 of the 229 that can be counted held; of the first eight, made before the ecosystem's behaviour was known, six were wrong, and none was revised. The most consequential defects were found not by tests the project wrote for itself but by running real integrations and by deliberately breaking each check to see whether a test noticed. A test that cannot fail proves nothing, and the evidence in this report is offered on that understanding.
